Paying a supplier in USDT or USDC does not automatically make an invoice "paid" in your books. A crypto invoice payment confirmation policy defines the specific, checkable condition — a confirmation count on-chain, or a settled desk ticket from an OTC provider — that your AP team relies on before changing invoice status. Without that written rule, two people can look at the same transaction and reach different conclusions about whether it's closed.

This matters because crypto payments don't have a single universal "cleared" moment the way a domestic bank transfer does. Blockchain transactions become final gradually, as blocks accumulate; OTC trades settle according to a desk's own operational timeline. Getting the rule wrong in either direction creates real exposure: closing invoices too early risks marking unpaid amounts as settled, while waiting indefinitely delays your [AP close](/blog/how-to-pay-business-invoices-with-usdt/) and frustrates vendors who are owed confirmation.

This piece sets out a practical, defensible framework finance and treasury leads can adopt — not a blockchain primer, and not a guide to chasing overpayments after the fact.

## Why "sent" is not the same as "paid"

A crypto transaction is broadcast, then included in a block, then followed by additional blocks that make reversal progressively less likely. In the time between broadcast and your policy's confirmation threshold, the transaction is pending — visible, but not yet treated as final under most internal controls.

For an invoice payment, this distinction has direct accounting consequences. If you record payment finality the moment a transaction is broadcast, you are booking a status your own policy doesn't yet support. If your controller or auditor later asks "what made this final," the answer needs to be a documented, repeatable rule — not "it looked settled in the wallet interface."

The same logic applies to OTC settlement. A trade desk ticket is not itself proof of delivery; the desk's own settlement confirmation — the point at which funds have moved and the desk marks the ticket settled — is the equivalent control point to an on-chain confirmation threshold. Businesses that route larger invoice payments through [OTC crypto desks](/blog/when-businesses-use-otc-crypto-desks/) need a parallel rule for ticket-based settlement, not just block-based settlement.

## Setting a confirmation threshold your policy can defend

Confirmation thresholds are typically calibrated to invoice value and network, not applied as one flat number across every payment. A low-value recurring invoice on a fast network may reasonably use fewer confirmations than a high-value one-off payment. What matters for defensibility is that the threshold is written down, applied consistently, and reviewed periodically — not that it matches any particular competitor's number.

A simple internal table can anchor this decision and gives auditors something concrete to review:

| Invoice value band | Suggested confirmation approach | Who signs off |
|---|---|---|
| Under $5,000 equivalent | Standard network confirmation threshold per policy | AP associate |
| $5,000–$50,000 equivalent | Higher confirmation threshold; hash logged in payment file | AP lead |
| Above $50,000 equivalent | Highest threshold or OTC desk settlement ticket required | Treasury + AP lead, dual sign-off |
| OTC desk settlement (any value) | Desk settlement notice, not block count | Treasury ops |

Thresholds should reference your organization's actual risk tolerance and the network used, and should be reviewed whenever you add a new settlement corridor or the underlying network's typical block time changes materially.

## Who verifies the transaction hash or desk ticket

Separation of duties is the control that makes crypto invoice payment confirmation defensible to an auditor. The person who initiates a payment should not be the same person who confirms it reached finality and updates invoice status. In practice, this usually breaks down as follows:

1. **Payment initiator** (treasury or AP) sends the payment and records the transaction hash or desk ticket number against the invoice.
2. **Independent reviewer** (a second AP team member or treasury ops) checks the hash on a block explorer, or the desk's settlement confirmation, against the required threshold.
3. **Amount reconciliation** confirms the received amount matches the invoice total under the agreed fee-handling rule (see below).
4. **Status change** is applied in the AP system only after steps 2 and 3 both pass.
5. **Audit trail entry** records who verified the payment, the timestamp, the hash or ticket ID, and the confirmation count or settlement notice referenced.

This sequence doesn't need specialized blockchain tooling — a documented process and a block explorer or the desk's own settlement portal are usually sufficient. What changes the risk profile is whether the sequence is followed every time, including for recurring vendors where teams are tempted to skip verification.

## When the network fee leaves the invoice short

Network fees are one of the most common reasons a crypto invoice payment confirmation process stalls. If a payment is sent for the exact invoice amount and a network fee is deducted somewhere in the flow — rather than added on top — the vendor can receive less than invoiced, even though the sender's intent was to pay in full.

This is a contractual and policy question before it's an accounting one. Your payment terms should state up front who bears network fees: the payer, the payee, or a shared/negotiated split. Three common approaches:

- **Payer absorbs the fee:** the invoice amount is sent plus an estimated fee buffer, so the vendor receives the full invoiced amount regardless of network conditions.
- **Fee deducted from invoice total:** the vendor receives invoice amount minus fee, and the invoice is only marked paid-in-full if a pre-agreed tolerance band covers the shortfall.
- **Fixed fee allowance in the contract:** vendor and payer agree in advance on a fee cap or fixed deduction amount, removing ambiguity at the point of settlement.

Whichever approach you use, the rule needs to be written into vendor payment terms, not decided invoice by invoice. An AP team without this rule will keep encountering small discrepancies that each require manual judgment — the kind of inconsistency an auditor flags even when individual amounts are immaterial.

## Writing the status back to the AP ledger

Once a payment clears your confirmation threshold and the amount reconciles against the invoice (accounting for any agreed fee handling), the status change itself needs a clean, auditable handoff into the ledger. This generally means:

- The invoice record is updated from "payment initiated" or "pending" to "paid," with a timestamp reflecting when the confirmation threshold was met — not when the payment was sent.
- The transaction hash or OTC settlement reference is attached permanently to the invoice record, not just logged in a separate payments spreadsheet.
- Any residual variance (fee-driven or otherwise) within your tolerance band is logged with a reason code, so recurring small variances don't appear as unexplained differences during reconciliation.
- Currency conversion used for the invoice's functional-currency value is recorded at the rate and source used at settlement, supporting consistent treatment for [corporate crypto treasury](/blog/corporate-crypto-treasury-basics/) reporting.

For businesses settling cross-border supplier invoices, this status handoff is also where confirmation-based settlement starts to look meaningfully different from a [bank wire](/blog/cross-border-crypto-settlement-vs-bank-wires/), where a single bank confirmation message typically suffices. Crypto payment confirmation requires the additional step of checking a threshold condition, which is why writing it into policy — rather than relying on ad hoc judgment — saves time at month-end.

## Risks and limits of a confirmation-based policy

A confirmation-threshold approach reduces ambiguity, but it does not eliminate operational risk, and no policy should be presented as a guarantee against loss or error. Some limits to document explicitly:

- Network congestion can delay confirmations beyond your typical close timeline, which may require a documented exception process rather than an ad hoc override.
- Desk settlement timing for OTC trades depends on the desk's own operational hours and processes; your policy should specify what happens if a ticket is still pending at your reporting cutoff.
- Exchange rate movement between invoice issuance and settlement, where invoices are denominated in [USDT or USDC](/blog/usdc-vs-usdt-for-supplier-payments/) versus fiat, needs a documented FX treatment separate from the confirmation question itself.
- Regulatory and accounting treatment of digital asset payments continues to evolve; teams preparing financial statements should review current guidance from standard-setters such as the IFRS Foundation (ifrs.org) and relevant national guidance rather than relying on a fixed internal assumption.
- AML and counterparty due diligence obligations — informed by frameworks such as the FATF standards (fatf-gafi.org) — sit alongside, not instead of, the payment confirmation check described here.

## Practical checklist before you mark an invoice paid

Use this as a working checklist for AP or treasury sign-off:

1. Confirm the transaction hash or OTC ticket number is logged against the correct invoice.
2. Verify the confirmation count or desk settlement status meets your documented threshold for that invoice value band.
3. Reconcile the received amount against the invoice total under your written fee-handling rule.
4. Confirm separation of duties — the reviewer is not the initiator.
5. Record the FX rate and source used, if the invoice is denominated differently from the settlement asset.
6. Attach the audit trail (hash, ticket ID, timestamp, reviewer name) to the invoice record before changing status.
7. Flag any variance outside tolerance for manual review rather than auto-closing the invoice.
