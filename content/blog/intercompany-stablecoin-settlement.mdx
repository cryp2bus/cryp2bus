Intercompany stablecoin settlement is how two legal entities within the same corporate group settle an internal invoice using a stablecoin, such as USDC or USDT, instead of a domestic or cross-border bank wire. One entity issues an internal invoice to another — for shared services, inventory transfers, or management fees — and the paying entity settles it from its own entity wallet to the receiving entity's wallet. Both sides then record the same transaction hash or over-the-counter (OTC) desk ticket number in their respective ledgers, which lets controllers match the entry during intercompany reconciliation without waiting on a bank confirmation.

This approach does not change what the invoice represents or how it should be taxed — it changes only the settlement rail. The documentation discipline below is what makes a stablecoin-settled intercompany invoice defensible to an auditor, just as a wire transfer confirmation would be.

## Why groups look at stablecoin settlement for intercompany invoices

Multi-entity groups often move value between subsidiaries for operational reasons: a shared-services entity invoices operating subsidiaries, a regional holding company invoices a local entity for allocated costs, or a group treasury center settles net balances after a cash-pooling cycle. When entities sit in different countries, a traditional bank wire can take one to several business days and pass through correspondent banks, each adding its own cut-off times and fees.

A stablecoin transfer between entity wallets settles on-chain in minutes, independent of banking hours in either jurisdiction. For groups that already use crypto rails for supplier payments — see [Pay business invoices with USDT](/blog/how-to-pay-business-invoices-with-usdt/) — extending the same rail to internal invoices avoids running two parallel settlement processes.

The key point for finance leads: speed is an operational benefit, not a reason to treat the transfer differently from any other intercompany settlement. The internal invoice remains the source document, and the approval and recording steps below apply regardless of settlement speed.

## Entity wallet structure: one wallet per legal entity

Before any transfer happens, group treasury needs to decide how wallets map to entities. The common and auditable pattern is one wallet per legal entity, not one shared group wallet.

| Structure | Description | Reconciliation impact |
|---|---|---|
| One wallet per entity | Each legal entity controls its own wallet and private key access | Balances and transfers map cleanly to each entity's ledger |
| Shared group wallet | A single wallet used across multiple entities | Requires manual sub-ledger allocation; harder to audit per entity |
| Wallet per entity + per currency | Separate wallets for USDC and USDT within each entity | Clean currency-level tracking, more addresses to manage |
| Omnibus treasury wallet with internal memo tags | One wallet, transfers tagged by memo or internal reference | Workable only with strict tagging discipline and dual review |

Most controllers prefer the first or third option because it keeps the entity wallet aligned with the entity's own general ledger, the same way a dedicated bank account would. If your group also settles with external suppliers in stablecoins, the choice between USDC and USDT for those flows is covered separately in [USDC vs USDT for supplier payments](/blog/usdc-vs-usdt-for-supplier-payments/), and the same stablecoin choice logic can apply internally for consistency across the group.

## The internal invoice as the source document

The internal invoice is the anchor document for intercompany stablecoin settlement, exactly as it would be for a bank-settled intercompany charge. It should include:

1. Invoice number and issue date, following the same numbering convention your group uses for other intercompany invoices.
2. Issuing entity and receiving entity, with full legal names and entity identifiers as registered.
3. Description of the underlying service, cost allocation, or goods transfer that the invoice represents.
4. Invoice amount in the group's functional or invoicing currency, with the stablecoin amount noted separately if conversion applies.
5. Payment terms and the settlement rail specified (stablecoin transfer, naming the wallet addresses involved).

Without this document, a stablecoin transfer between two entity wallets is just a blockchain transaction with no accounting meaning. The invoice is what turns the transfer into a recorded, auditable event tied to a specific commercial or cost-sharing arrangement within the group.

## Who approves the transfer

A clear approval chain matters more for intercompany stablecoin settlement than for routine bank transfers, because blockchain transfers are generally irreversible once confirmed. A typical control structure separates three roles:

| Role | Responsibility | Typical control |
|---|---|---|
| Invoice originator | Raises the internal invoice in the accounting system | Invoice matches underlying cost allocation or service agreement |
| Treasury approver | Reviews the invoice and releases the stablecoin transfer from the paying entity's wallet | Dual approval above a defined threshold; verifies receiving wallet address |
| Controller | Reconciles both entities' ledgers against the transaction hash or desk ticket | Confirms amount, date, and invoice reference match on both sides |

For larger intercompany transfers, or when a group routes the settlement through an OTC desk to convert between currencies or manage size, the desk ticket number serves the same documentary role as the blockchain hash. Groups that already use desks for sizeable external transfers can read more on desk selection in [When businesses use OTC crypto desks](/blog/when-businesses-use-otc-crypto-desks/) — the same desk relationship can be used for internal transfers where it makes operational sense.

## Matching the record: transaction hash or desk ticket on both ledgers

This is the step that makes intercompany stablecoin settlement auditable. Once the transfer executes, both entities need the same reference recorded against the same invoice:

- The paying entity records a debit to the intercompany payable account and a credit to its entity wallet, referencing the internal invoice number and the transaction hash (or desk ticket, if a desk executed the transfer).
- The receiving entity records a credit to the intercompany receivable account and a debit to its entity wallet, referencing the same invoice number and the same hash or ticket.
- The controller running group-level intercompany reconciliation matches both entries using the shared reference, the invoice number plus the hash or ticket, rather than relying on bank statement line items.

This mirrors how accounting standards generally expect supporting documentation for any transaction, including digital asset transactions — see the IASB's published materials on accounting for crypto-assets for broader context on recognition and measurement questions at ifrs.org. Cryptobus does not provide accounting advice; your controller and auditor should confirm how your group's chosen accounting framework treats stablecoin balances and transfers.

## Risks and limits of intercompany stablecoin settlement

Intercompany stablecoin settlement reduces reliance on correspondent banking for internal flows, but it does not remove operational or compliance considerations:

- **Irreversibility.** A transfer sent to the wrong wallet address generally cannot be reversed, which is why address verification sits inside the treasury approver's control, not left to the person executing the transfer.
- **Wallet security.** Entity wallets holding meaningful balances need the same access controls, key management, and segregation of duties as a bank account signatory list.
- **Stablecoin issuer and counterparty risk.** The value of a stablecoin transfer depends on the issuer maintaining its peg and operational reserves; group treasury should understand which stablecoins it holds and why, rather than treating all stablecoins as interchangeable.
- **No guaranteed settlement value.** Cryptobus does not promise any return, yield, or guaranteed outcome from holding or transferring stablecoins between entities; this is a settlement mechanism, not an investment strategy.
- **Jurisdictional rules differ.** AML and reporting expectations for crypto transactions vary by country; FATF's guidance at fatf-gafi.org and, for groups with EU entities, the official EU materials on the Markets in Crypto-Assets (MiCA) framework are useful starting points for understanding applicable requirements in each entity's jurisdiction.
- **This is not tax or consolidation guidance.** Intercompany stablecoin settlement documents how an invoice is paid; it does not determine transfer pricing treatment, tax consolidation entries, or elimination of intercompany balances at the group level. Those remain separate workstreams for your tax and consolidation teams.

## Practical checklist for documenting intercompany stablecoin settlement

Before your group settles its first intercompany invoice in stablecoins, confirm each of the following:

1. Each participating entity has its own dedicated wallet, with access controls matching your existing signatory policy.
2. The internal invoice exists in your accounting system before the transfer is initiated, not drafted retroactively.
3. A named treasury approver verifies the receiving wallet address against a pre-approved address list before releasing funds.
4. The transaction hash (or OTC desk ticket number) is captured and attached to the invoice record immediately after execution.
5. Both entities' controllers reconcile their ledgers against the same hash or ticket within the same reporting period.
6. Your group has decided, in writing, how stablecoin balances are reported in each entity's functional currency for internal reporting purposes.
7. Someone outside the originating and approving roles periodically reviews completed intercompany stablecoin transfers as part of your internal controls.
