Crypto vendor master data is the internally controlled set of payee fields that accounts payable keeps on file for a supplier paid in digital assets — separate from, and checked against, whatever payment instructions appear on a given invoice. Before any crypto payment is released, a mature AP process confirms seven things: legal entity name, asset, network, beneficiary wallet address, memo or tag (if the network requires one), the date the record was last verified, and the named internal owner of that record. This article is not an onboarding questionnaire for new suppliers and not a list of what belongs on an invoice. It is a field-by-field guide to what should already sit in your vendor master file, and how AP checks an incoming invoice against that file before authorizing a transfer.

## What Crypto Vendor Master Data Means for Accounts Payable

In traditional AP, vendor master data usually means a legal name, a tax ID, and a bank account or IBAN, stored once and referenced on every subsequent invoice. Crypto vendor master data follows the same logic but with different fields, because a wrong character in a wallet address does not bounce back the way a mistyped IBAN often does. Once a transaction confirms on-chain, reversal is not routinely available.

That single operational fact is why the master record matters more here than in fiat AP. The invoice tells you what the supplier is billing. The master record tells you where, on which network, and under what name you are authorized to send funds. AP's job before release is to confirm those two sources agree — not to treat the invoice as the source of truth for payment routing.

Teams that already pay suppliers in stablecoins, as described in [Pay business invoices with USDT](/blog/how-to-pay-business-invoices-with-usdt/), typically formalize this split early: one file for "what is owed," one file for "where it is paid," with controls that prevent either from silently overriding the other.

## The Core Fields: Legal Entity Name, Asset, Network, Address, Memo

A usable crypto vendor master record needs a fixed set of fields, stored consistently across every payee. Missing any one of them turns a routine release into a manual investigation at the worst possible time — right before a payment deadline.

1. **Legal entity name** — the registered name of the counterparty, matched to the entity named on the contract or invoice, not a trading name or a shortened version used in email signatures.
2. **Asset** — the specific token the vendor is to be paid in (for example USDT or USDC), since the same vendor may hold wallets for more than one asset and substituting one for another without authorization is a control failure, not a convenience.
3. **Network** — the blockchain the address belongs to (Ethereum, Tron, Polygon, and others), because the same-looking address format can exist on multiple networks with no cross-network recovery if funds are sent on the wrong one. Organizations weighing which stablecoin and network combination to standardize on often start from the comparison in [USDC vs USDT for supplier payments](/blog/usdc-vs-usdt-for-supplier-payments/).
4. **Beneficiary wallet address** — the full destination address, stored in a format that supports copy-paste verification rather than manual retyping, with no abbreviation.
5. **Memo or destination tag** — required on some networks (certain exchange-hosted accounts, for instance) and genuinely not required on others; the field should record which case applies, not be left empty by default.
6. **Last verification date** — the date this specific record was confirmed accurate, through a defined verification step, not the date the vendor was first onboarded.
7. **Record owner** — the named individual or role accountable for that verification and for processing any future change request on the record.

## Matching Vendor Master Data Against the Invoice

Before AP releases a crypto payment, the invoice and the vendor master record go through a comparison, not a one-way lookup. The invoice supplies the amount and the billing reference; the master record supplies the destination. The table below shows how each master field is used at release time.

| Master field | What it is checked against | Action if mismatched |
|---|---|---|
| Legal entity name | Name on the invoice and underlying contract | Hold release, confirm entity identity before proceeding |
| Asset | Currency/token specified on the invoice | Hold release, do not substitute assets without written authorization |
| Network | Any network reference on the invoice or prior correspondence | Hold release, confirm network with the owner of record |
| Beneficiary wallet address | Address shown in the current invoice, if any is printed | Flag as a change request; do not auto-update the master record |
| Memo or tag | Any memo/tag printed on the invoice | Hold release, memo mismatches can route funds to an unintended sub-account |
| Last verification date | Internal policy threshold (e.g., 90–180 days) | If stale, trigger re-verification before release, regardless of invoice content |

This matching step is where most documented crypto payment losses trace back to a process gap rather than a technology failure: an invoice arrives with a new address, AP updates the master record on the strength of the invoice alone, and the new address belongs to an attacker rather than the vendor. Vendor master data is supposed to prevent exactly that by requiring a separate, defined verification path for any change — never the invoice itself as the trigger for updating payment routing.

Teams running higher transaction volumes or cross-border supplier bases often formalize this matching logic inside their broader treasury controls, an approach outlined in [Corporate crypto treasury basics](/blog/corporate-crypto-treasury-basics/), and extend similar controls to large one-off settlements handled through [When businesses use OTC crypto desks](/blog/when-businesses-use-otc-crypto-desks/).

## Who Owns the Record and When It Was Last Verified

Two fields in the table above carry disproportionate operational weight: record owner and last verification date. Neither is cosmetic.

The record owner is the named person or role who can answer, on demand, "how was this address confirmed, and when?" Without a named owner, change requests tend to get actioned by whoever happens to be processing invoices that week, which removes accountability exactly where it is most needed. Ownership should be documented in the vendor master file itself, not inferred from an org chart.

The last verification date tells AP whether the record is still within policy before relying on it. A record verified fourteen months ago, with no re-check since, should not clear a six-figure release simply because the address field is populated. Many finance teams tie this date to a fixed interval — commonly 90 to 180 days — and to a mandatory re-check triggered by any communication claiming to update vendor details, regardless of channel.

## A Release Checklist for AP Teams

Before authorizing any crypto payment against an existing vendor master record, AP should confirm:

1. Legal entity name on the invoice matches the legal entity name in the master record exactly.
2. Asset specified on the invoice matches the asset field in the master record.
3. Network matches, with no assumption carried over from a previous payment to the same vendor.
4. Beneficiary wallet address in the master record is used for payment — never an address copied directly from the current invoice without a separate verification step.
5. Memo or tag field is checked and, where applicable, applied correctly to the transaction.
6. Last verification date falls within the organization's defined policy window.
7. Any discrepancy between invoice and master record is escalated to the named record owner before release, not resolved unilaterally by the person processing the payment.

This sequence does not replace broader settlement procedures; it sits upstream of them. For cross-border suppliers, the same master-record discipline applies whether the comparison is against a bank wire corridor or an on-chain settlement path, a distinction covered in [Cross-border crypto vs bank wires](/blog/cross-border-crypto-settlement-vs-bank-wires/).

## Risks and Limits of Vendor Master Data for Crypto Payees

Vendor master data reduces, but does not eliminate, payment-routing risk. A few limits are worth stating plainly.

First, a well-maintained record is only as reliable as the verification method used to populate it. If "verification" means a reply-all email confirming an address, the record inherits all the weaknesses of email-based confirmation, including impersonation.

Second, master data governance does not substitute for network-level checks at the point of transfer, such as confirming the destination address format matches the selected network before broadcasting a transaction.

Third, no field in the master record can compensate for the irreversible nature of on-chain settlement once a transaction confirms. The entire purpose of pre-release checks is to catch errors before that point, not after.

Finally, vendor master data standards vary by organization and are not currently mandated by a single global regulator. Guidance from bodies such as the Financial Action Task Force (fatf-gafi.org) addresses anti-money-laundering expectations around virtual asset transfers generally, and accounting treatment references from the IFRS Foundation (ifrs.org) address how digital assets are reported, but neither prescribes a specific vendor master field schema. The structure described here reflects common AP practice, not a regulatory requirement.
