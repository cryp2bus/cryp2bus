Sanctions rescreening for repeat crypto suppliers is the control that keeps a one-time "pass" from quietly becoming a permanent exemption. A vendor cleared at onboarding is not cleared forever — ownership changes, lists update, and payout wallets get swapped. For a supplier you pay regularly in USDT, USDC, or another digital asset, rescreening means re-running the sanctions and watchlist check on a set cadence, plus immediately whenever a defined trigger fires, such as a wallet change, a new settlement corridor, or a change in beneficial ownership. The result — clear, potential match, or escalation — gets filed against the vendor's record with a date and list version, and the related payable pauses until any open match is resolved.

This article lays out a checklist-style approach: cadence, triggers, filing, and pause conditions. It does not reproduce a general AML control map, and it does not list restricted jurisdictions — those belong in your sanctions policy and primary-source lists, not in an educational explainer.

## Why repeat payees need a refresh, not just onboarding

Initial KYB and sanctions screening answer one question: was this entity clear on the day you onboarded it? That answer decays. Ownership structures change, intermediaries get added, and designated-persons lists are updated by regulators on an ongoing basis — not on your onboarding schedule.

For businesses settling invoices in crypto, there's an additional layer: the payout wallet itself. A supplier can keep the same legal entity while rotating the address it asks you to pay. If your rescreening only covers the corporate name and never touches the wallet or corridor, you can miss exactly the kind of change that matters most for payment risk. If you're building or reviewing a supplier payment process, see our overview on [paying business invoices with USDT](/blog/how-to-pay-business-invoices-with-usdt/) for how wallet and settlement data typically enter the vendor file in the first place.

FATF's standards on virtual assets and the broader AML framework (https://www.fatf-gafi.org) treat ongoing due diligence as a continuing obligation, not a one-time gate. Your internal policy is where that principle becomes a concrete cadence and trigger list.

## Setting a rescreening cadence for crypto suppliers

A defensible cadence usually ties refresh frequency to a risk tier assigned at onboarding — based on factors like corridor, transaction volume, and entity type. There is no regulator-mandated universal number; the table below reflects common ranges used in payment ops programs rather than a fixed requirement.

| Risk tier | Typical refresh interval | Example trigger sensitivity |
|---|---|---|
| Low (domestic, small recurring invoices) | 180–365 days | Standard trigger list applies |
| Medium (cross-border, moderate volume) | 90–180 days | Lower threshold for "unusual amount" trigger |
| High (new corridor, higher-risk counterparties) | 30–90 days | Any wallet or ownership change reviewed immediately |

Document the tier assignment and the interval in the vendor's compliance file, not just in a policy PDF. When an auditor or your own QA function asks why Vendor X was last screened on a given date, the answer should be traceable to a stated rule, not a memory.

## Trigger events that force an off-cycle rescreen

Cadence covers the routine refresh. Trigger events force an off-cycle check regardless of where the vendor sits in its cycle. For repeat crypto payees, the following are the trigger categories most teams build into their workflow:

1. **Wallet or payout address change** — a new destination address submitted for an existing vendor, even if the legal entity name is unchanged.
2. **Corridor change** — a shift in the originating or receiving jurisdiction, intermediary VASP, or settlement network used for the payment.
3. **Ownership or control change** — a reported change in beneficial ownership, directors, or corporate structure disclosed by the vendor or found in a routine KYB refresh.
4. **Unusual transaction pattern** — an invoice amount, frequency, or structuring pattern that departs materially from the vendor's established baseline.
5. **External alert** — a list update, adverse media hit, or notification from a screening vendor tied to the payee's name, entity, or known wallet cluster.

Any one of these should queue an immediate rescreen rather than waiting for the next scheduled cycle. A vendor refresh triggered by a wallet change is the most common blind spot in crypto payment ops — treasury teams track the invoice and the amount carefully but don't always route a changed wallet address back through compliance before the next payment goes out.

## Where the rescreening result gets filed

A rescreen that isn't documented is functionally the same as a rescreen that never happened, from an audit perspective. The filed record should capture, at minimum:

- The date and time of the rescreen
- The list or database version used (sanctions, PEP, adverse media)
- Whether the trigger was cadence-based or event-based, and which event
- The outcome: clear, potential match requiring review, or confirmed match
- Who closed the review and what evidence supported the decision
- Any linked wallet, corridor, or ownership data that changed since the last screen

This record attaches to the same vendor file used for onboarding KYB, not a separate spreadsheet that nobody reconciles. If your organization also evaluates settlement assets side by side — for example when deciding between stablecoins for a given corridor — the vendor file is a natural place to keep that context linked, as discussed in [USDC vs USDT for supplier payments](/blog/usdc-vs-usdt-for-supplier-payments/).

## When a payable must pause

Pausing a payment is an operational decision, not a punitive one — it buys time to close an open question before funds move. A payable should pause when:

1. A rescreen returns an unresolved potential match that hasn't been dispositioned.
2. A trigger event (wallet, corridor, ownership) has occurred but the corresponding rescreen hasn't been completed yet.
3. Supporting documentation for a wallet or corridor change request hasn't been received or verified.
4. The vendor's risk tier has been escalated pending a fuller review.
5. An external alert names the vendor, a related entity, or a known-associated wallet address and the match hasn't been ruled out.

The pause lifts once the review is closed and logged — not before. ## Building the rescreening workflow into ops

A practical rescreening workflow usually has five working parts:

1. **Tiering at onboarding** — assign a risk tier that sets the default refresh interval.
2. **Calendar-driven cadence checks** — a scheduled job or ops task that flags vendors due for refresh.
3. **Trigger intake** — a clear channel (ticket, form, or system field) for anyone who learns of a wallet, corridor, or ownership change to route it to compliance before the next payment.
4. **Disposition and filing** — a standard template for recording the outcome against the vendor file.
5. **Payment hold integration** — the payables system checks vendor status before release, so an open rescreen automatically blocks disbursement rather than relying on someone remembering to check.

When payments cross borders, the corridor itself adds variables worth tracking alongside the vendor file — see [cross-border crypto vs bank wires](/blog/cross-border-crypto-settlement-vs-bank-wires/) for how settlement rails differ in practice.

## Risks and limits

Rescreening reduces exposure to sanctions and counterparty risk, but it does not eliminate it. List updates can lag real-world designations; a vendor can be clear on a given list version and still be connected to an undisclosed related party. Wallet-level visibility also has limits — blockchain analytics can flag known clusters but cannot guarantee attribution for every address, and a vendor may use an intermediary that obscures the ultimate beneficiary. None of this process guarantees that a supplier relationship is risk-free, and none of it should be read as investment or legal advice. Your own sanctions policy, your jurisdiction's regulatory requirements, and your counsel's guidance take precedence over any general framework described here. Outcomes depend on how consistently the cadence and triggers are actually applied, not just on having a policy document.

Reference frameworks such as FATF's guidance (https://www.fatf-gafi.org) and, where relevant, EU-level materials on crypto-asset service providers under MiCA, are useful for benchmarking a policy — they are not a substitute for your own legal review.
